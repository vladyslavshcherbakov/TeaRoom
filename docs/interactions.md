# Interactions

An action in Tea Room is specified as behaviour, not as a feature. A mechanic is designed only when every field below has an answer. "The player can pour water" is not a design.

## The contract of an interaction

| Field | Question it answers |
|---|---|
| Actor | Who acts: the player, the cat, a guest, the world. |
| Target | What is acted on. |
| Preconditions | When the action is available at all. |
| Input | Which gesture starts it. |
| Continuous behaviour | What happens while the gesture lasts. |
| Feedback | What the player sees, hears and feels. |
| Completion | When the action counts as done. |
| Interruption | What happens when the finger lifts early or the player changes their mind. |
| Imperfection | What a clumsy version produces. |
| State change | Which values in the world changed. |
| Aftereffect | What lingers right after: drops, steam, a clink. |
| Intent | The feeling the player should keep. |

Definition of done for a new mechanic: a gesture, a visual response, a continuous response, a sound, a state change, a completion, an interruption, an imperfect outcome, persistence where the world should remember it, a consequence elsewhere in the room, and a mobile-safe touch target.

## Touch profiles

Objects share one physics profile per weight class, so mass is felt through the finger.

| Class | Examples | Follow lag | Lift | Scale on grab |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Light | bowl | low | 5 px | +3% |
| Medium | caddy, thermos | medium | 4 px | +3% |
| Heavy | kettle | high | 2–3 px | +2% |

Grab: `pointerdown`, then 100–150 ms without movement. The lift animation takes 120–180 ms. Nothing counts as moved before `pointerup`. On release the object snaps to a matching socket if it is over one, stays where it was put if the spot is allowed, and springs back to where it came from if the spot is forbidden. A spot is forbidden when any part of the item would be over another item, the kettle's spout included, while the spout may hang past an edge. There is no red highlight and no error text.

Animation always goes anticipation, action, settle. A bowl: lift, move, descend, contact, settle. A lid: grip, lift, rotate, settle. Water: tilt, stream, transfer, final drops, stop.

## Interactions and the simulation

The simulation core receives one command per player decision and answers with events. A refused command is an `actionRefused` event with a reason, and the presentation shows it materially: the object springs back, the lid stays shut.

| Interaction | Gesture | Commands | Events the presentation reacts to |
|---|---|---|---|
| Walk to a place | tap a piece of furniture, the keeper arrives | `standAt` with the place, or with none when walking away | `keeperMoved` |
| Take an item | tap it in a close-up | `pickUp` | `pickedUp` with the hand |
| Put an item down | select it in a hand, then tap a surface | `putDown` with the exact spot | `putDown` |
| Choose the mood | pick presets before or during the ritual | `chooseAtmosphere` | `atmosphereChanged` |
| Begin | pick a tea | `beginRitual` | `ritualBegan` |
| Open or close a lid | tap the lid | `openVesselLid`, `closeVesselLid` | `vesselLidOpened`, `vesselLidClosed` |
| Put the kettle on the heater | choose the kettle's hand, then tap the heater | `placeOnHeater` | `placedOnHeater` |
| Lift the kettle off | tap the kettle on the heater | `pickUp` | `takenOffHeater` with a water judgement if the heater was on, then `pickedUp` |
| Fill in the sink | choose the kettle's hand, tap the sink, tap the tap to turn it on and open the kettle's lid; tap the tap again to turn it off, and tap the kettle to take it | `putInTheSink`, `turnTheTapOn`, `turnTheTapOff` | `putInTheSink`, `tapTurnedOn`, `vesselOverflowed`, `tapTurnedOff` |
| Heat | tap the switch on the counter's front | `switchHeaterOn`, `switchHeaterOff` | `heaterSwitchedOn`, `targetTemperatureReached`, `heaterSwitchedOff` with a water judgement |

| Pour | choose the hand with the vessel, tap the target, then move the vessel with one finger and hold the tilt button with another | `startPouring`, `adjustPour` on every tilt change, `stopPouring` | `pourStarted`, `vesselOverflowed`, `pourFinished` with poured and spilled millilitres |
| Scoop leaves | take the spoon, choose its hand, then tap the open caddy | `scoopTea` with a full spoon's depth | `teaScooped` |
| Tip leaves | with the spoon's hand chosen, tap the open kettle | `tipSpoonInto` | `leavesAdded`, then `brewStarted` once leaves and water meet |

| Offer | choose the hand with a tea bowl, then tap a figurine | `offerCup` | `figurineAcceptedTea` with a response |

| Soak | put the cloth down in the puddle on the tea table | `putDown`, then `soakUpThePuddle` with the cloth | `putDown`, `clothLaidInThePuddle` with the cloth |

| Leave | tap "Leave" while resting | `leaveRoom` | `roomLeft` |

## Gestures in the room

A touch that moves less than 12 px and lasts under half a second is a tap.

A press that moves less than 12 px is a tap when the finger lifts, unless it started a pour or became a hold. A press that moves further does nothing. A press on an item the keeper holds, or on its lid, that stays within 12 px for a second is a hold: it shows the item up close, and its finger lifting is not a tap. Moving further, or a second finger, turns it back into an ordinary press, and a finger lifted earlier is a tap.

| Gesture | What it does |
|---|---|
| Tap the floor | The keeper walks there, around the furniture. |
| Tap a piece of furniture, or anything on it | The keeper walks to it, and the camera shows it close up. The tea table can be used from both long sides: the keeper takes the shorter way, to the front or to the window side, and the camera shows the table from the side the keeper stands at. From the window side it looks from behind the back wall, so the wall and the sill fill the bottom of the screen. |
| Tap the floor or empty space in a close-up | The camera returns to the room. |
| Pinch with two fingers, or turn the mouse wheel | The camera moves closer or farther along its line of sight, from half to 1.6 times its usual distance. The first finger's press does not count as a tap. The room keeps its zoom while the keeper walks to furniture, and gets it back after the close-up. Each close-up starts at its usual distance, and its own zoom is forgotten when the player leaves it. There is no pinch while a pour is being aimed. |
| Tap a rose bush in the garden ten times in a row | The debug menu opens. Any other tap before the tenth starts the count again. The menu switches the camera between the room view and first person, chooses which stick walks and which looks, and closes with its Close button. |
| In first person, push the walking stick, on the left unless the debug menu swaps it | The keeper walks the way the stick points, at walking speed, only on the room's floor, and slides along the furniture and the walls instead of passing through them. Walking away from a piece of furniture leaves it, and walking stops a walk to furniture that a tap started. |
| In first person, push the looking stick, on the right unless the debug menu swaps it | The view turns all the way round, faster the further the stick is pushed, and tilts up and down up to about sixty degrees. |
| In first person, tap anything | The same as in the room view: the keeper walks there while the view turns smoothly towards the way, furniture shows its close-up, and leaving the close-up glides back into first person. |
| Tap an item in a close-up | A bowl, the spoon and the cloth answer a tap in an invisible area half as wide again as they are, so a small item at the back of a shelf is easy to hit. The keeper takes it into the first free hand, and that hand is chosen at once, so the item can be used or put back with the next tap. A soft warm glow, twice as wide as the item, breathes slowly behind the chosen item in its corner, so the player sees which item a tap will use. With both hands full, the item stays where it is, and the keeper answers each try with one line that changes every time: about growing a third arm or learning telekinesis, or, when both hands hold tea bowls, about learning to carry several bowls in one hand. The tenth tap in a row on the same item with both hands full grows a middle hand that takes it and is chosen. A tap on anything else starts the count again. In a close-up its item is held low in the middle of the view, above the Sip button, and in the room before the keeper's chest. It is used like any hand, and it is gone once its item is put anywhere. It grows only once: after that, ten taps get the usual line, until the achievements are reset. |
| Tap an item the keeper holds, in a bottom corner of a close-up | That hand is chosen, and its item rises a little. A second tap lets go of the choice. Leaving the close-up lets go of it too, and in the room nothing is chosen: a tap on the keeper's hands does nothing there. A tap on the touch area around an item, standing or held, but not on the item itself, reaches any item seen behind it, whether a hand is chosen or not, so a bowl standing behind another bowl is aimed at and taken as it is seen. The touch area around the chosen item also lets a tap through to what lies behind it when the chosen item can act on it: a surface to put it down on, the heater, the tap, a figurine, or a vessel to tip leaves into or pour into. A tap on the floor or on nothing there still lets go of the choice. |
| Hold a finger still on an item the keeper holds, or on its lid, for a second | The item comes up close: large in the middle of the screen, in front of the eyes, lit by a soft light from the viewer's upper left, over the room and the other held items dimmed behind it. The hold works on either hand and on the middle hand, chosen or not, in a close-up, in first person and in the room view, but not while a pour is aimed. The finger that held may drag on at once to turn the item. The camera stays where it is, the Sip button and the sticks hide, and the world goes on: water heats, tea cools and steeps. Nothing about the item changes: its water, tea, leaves and lid show as they do in the hand. |
| Drag one finger while an item is up close | The item turns under the finger, all the way round both ways: across the screen it turns around its own upright, up and down it tips towards or away from the viewer. A hundred pixels turn it one radian. It starts tipped towards the viewer as in the hand. |
| Pinch, or turn the mouse wheel, while an item is up close | The item grows or shrinks, from six tenths to three times the size it came up at. The camera's zoom does not change. |
| Tap while an item is up close | A tap on the item or its lid does nothing. A tap anywhere else puts the item back in its hand and does nothing more: it does not walk, put anything down or choose a hand, even on another held item. The hand that was chosen before the hold is chosen again, and no other. |
| Tap a surface in a close-up with a hand chosen | The item goes down exactly where the finger touched, if it fits. It does not fit over the edge, on the heater, on another item or on top of the shelf, which is not a place for things, and then it stays in the hand, still chosen. Once in a visit, when the last item that was elsewhere goes onto the shelf and everything the keeper can carry stands there, the keeper remarks on the tidiness in one line. |
| Tap the heater with a hand chosen | The kettle, the thermos, the cloth or the spoon in that hand goes on the heater. On a working heater the thermos's metal darkens and glows red, and while it glows a tap on it or on its lid leaves it as it is, and the keeper says in one line that it is too hot to take. A tea bowl or the caddy stays in the hand, and the keeper objects in one line that changes with every try: the bowl is the dearest thing in the house, and the caddy's tea was roasted long ago. Once in a visit, when the player has tried the fourth or fifth different item on the working heater, whether it went on or not, tries on a cold heater aside, the keeper teases them in place of that item's own line, with one of six lines: a tester, an arsonist, a room about to be renamed. The count, four or five, is chosen at random when the page opens. Every later try gets the item's own line again. |
| Tap the switch under the heater | The heater switches on or off, whatever the hands hold and whichever is chosen. The plate glows while it is on. A tap on the switch through the chosen hand's item reaches the switch. Switched off after it worked two minutes in all with nothing on it or with anything but the kettle, it earns one line from the keeper about the kilowatt-hours it wasted. |
| Tap the lid of a kettle, thermos or caddy, standing or held in a corner of the close-up | The lid opens or closes. An open lid lies on the surface beside its item where there is room, trying the left, the right, the front, the back and then the diagonals, never on the heater, over the sink, past the edge or on another item, and with no room it stands open on its hinge at the rim. Nothing can be put down on an open lid lying there. On an item in the sink or in a hand, the lid stands open on its hinge at the rim, swung a little past upright, so water falling into the opening never meets it. Taking an item closes its lid. |
| Tap the spoon or the cloth | The keeper takes it into a free hand, like any item. It shows in a corner of the close-up and is put down like any item. |
| Tap the open caddy with the spoon's hand chosen | A full spoon of leaves is scooped, and the leaves show on the spoon. |
| Tap a vessel with the spoon's hand chosen | The spoon's leaves are tipped into it. The kettle and the tea bowls take leaves, so tea can be brewed right in a bowl. With an empty spoon there is nothing to tip, so the vessel is taken into the free hand, as is any other item. |
| Tap Sip, shown while the chosen hand holds a tea bowl or the caddy with something in it | The keeper takes a sip and says in one line how it feels, for nine seconds, or until the player taps the line away: too hot, bitter, thin, just right. Plain water with no tea in it gets its own line. Each feeling has three or four phrases, and each visit gets its own voice, chosen at random when the page opens: the same phrase for the same feeling during a visit, and after a reload another voice may say another. A sip of heavy or extreme tea straight from the caddy kills the keeper: the screen fades behind a dark band with YOU DIED in red letters, as in Dark Souls. The keeper's last words, one of three lines written for dying, follow under it in red italics, then an obituary that never names the keeper, and after four seconds a Start over button reloads the game from the start, with the same line under it as on the screen that offers to continue a visit. None of it fades while the screen is up. |
| Tap a figurine with a hand chosen | In the close-up of the tea table, the tea bowl in that hand is offered to it. The figurines stand on the sill behind the table, so from anywhere else a tap on them leaves the keeper where they are, and the room answers with a line that it keeps the sill for itself. The line changes with every such tap. A caption shows the figurine's response. |
| Stroke a table or the counter with the cloth's hand chosen | The cloth moves under the finger, and that surface is wiped as it goes, so the puddle shrinks while the finger moves. Each 2 cm piece of the stroke is wiped at its own speed. Only the part of a piece where the cloth is over the puddle counts, and the share it covers is that length times the cloth's 20 cm width over the puddle's area. A slow stroke dries more. The cloth darkens as it takes in water and dries again by itself. A tap on a surface puts the cloth down, like any item, anywhere it fits, and a tap on another item takes that item into the free hand. Put down in the puddle, it soaks it up while it lies there: the puddle shrinks slowly, and the cloth darkens as it takes the water in. |
| Tap the sink with a hand chosen | That hand's item goes into the empty sink, and the tap stays as it was. With nothing chosen, a tap on the sink does nothing. |
| Tap the tap | The water turns on or off, whatever the hands hold and whichever is chosen. Running onto a vessel with its lid closed, it runs over the lid down the drain, and the player opens the lid in the sink, by tapping it, to let the water in. The kettle's water gauge rises. The water keeps running while the keeper is away. A tap on the item in the sink takes it back into a hand, with its lid closed, and the tap keeps running until it is turned off. The tap answers a tap anywhere in an invisible box around its post and arm, about as wide as a fingertip on a phone. A vessel seen behind that box still wins the tap, and so does the sink under it. A tap on the tap through the chosen hand's item reaches the tap. |
| Tap a vessel on a surface with the hand of another vessel chosen, when the chosen vessel holds something | The pour begins to be aimed: the chosen vessel hovers over the target, 22 cm to its left on the screen with its spout turned towards it, so it is never hidden behind the furniture, and a round tilt button with a teapot appears. The pour goes into whichever vessel standing on that furniture the spout is over: moving the spout over another bowl makes it the target, and a pour already running carries on into it. The first time, a short note explains the gesture: drag to move, hold the teapot button to tilt, tap anywhere to stop. It is not shown again on that device. |
| Drag a finger anywhere while aiming | The vessel moves with the finger, keeping its height above the target. Only the change of the finger's position counts, so the finger never hides the vessel. A tall vessel such as the thermos is held higher, so its base never sinks into the surface, however it tilts. |
| Hold the tilt button while aiming | The vessel tilts by 30° a second up to 37°, just below the tilt where a tenth of the stream would splash, so a steady pour into the middle of a bowl keeps the table dry. Past 10° it pours. The share of the stream that lands inside the target's opening goes in, the rest falls on the table. Releasing the button tilts the vessel back by 70° a second, and the pour stops when it is upright. A pour that spilled 5 ml or more gets a line from the keeper. |
| Tap anywhere while aiming, without dragging | The aiming ends. On a free spot of a surface the vessel is put down there, anywhere else it returns to its hand. |
| Tap the lid of the vessel being aimed | The lid opens or closes, and the aiming goes on. A thermos aimed with its lid closed pours once its lid is opened this way. |

The keeper never tells the same joke twice in a visit. The lines of one kind, such as the answers to full hands, to a bowl held over the heater, to the figurines tapped from afar, to a thermos too hot to take, or to a long-running tap or heater, come one at a time, each different. Once every line of that kind has been said, the keeper keeps quiet about it until the next visit. The lines about the cloth, taken off the heater while it smoulders or washed back to new, are said once a visit. A spill of 5 ml or more gets a line, a different one each time, but at most once in two minutes of play, so the keeper does not comment on every slip. The sip lines and the figurines' responses are not jokes, and they are said every time.

A gold medal on a red ribbon hangs on the wall between the counter and the shelf. A tap on it opens a sheet with sixteen achievements, and a tap outside the sheet or on Close shuts it. Every title shows from the start, and a title only hints at what earns it. An achievement earned is crossed out and ticked, and a line under it says what the player did. An achievement not yet earned that cannot be earned in this room or at this moment is dimmed, with no word on why: the prophecy in a room without the back window, the tea achievements once no tea leaves are left, dying of tea from the caddy once the caddy and the spoon hold no leaves, and the crumbled spoon while the spoon is gone. When one is earned, its title shows at the top of the screen for four seconds, one after another when several come at once. Achievements are kept in the browser for good: a new visit, a continued one, death and Start over all keep them. Reset on the sheet clears them all, after a second tap to confirm. They are earned by burning the cloth and washing it back to new, crumbling the spoon, trying a tea bowl on the heater, putting everything away on the shelf, wiping two different puddles, heating the thermos red, the heater tester's tease, ten taps on the rose bush, a sip that is just right, a sip from a tea bowl with leaves brewed in it, continuing a visit that began after the achievements came to the game, so a visit saved by an older version does not count, a tap run two minutes into the empty sink and then turned off, and a heater that heated the air or things not made for it, anything but the kettle, for two minutes in all and was then switched off by the player, in any visits, waiting until the kettle boils dry, growing the middle hand, dying of tea from the caddy, and seeing the whole prophecy on the screen, facing it with nothing in front of it.

A steel gear hangs right under the medal. A tap on it, or a few centimetres around it, opens the settings, and a tap outside the sheet or on Close shuts it. Body colour offers eight colours in round swatches, and a tap on one paints the keeper's body at once. Face offers, in words and without pictures, one of four: a nose, two dark eyes, two ears, or a big curly afro. The last one reads only Hair, so the hairstyle is a surprise. Only one shows at a time. Each new game picks the nose, the eyes or the ears at random, and a continued visit keeps it. Hair is never picked: the player finds it in the settings. Under Advanced, Soft shadows in corners darkens the corners and the places where things meet, so the room looks deeper. It is off at first, and a warning under it says that the game may start to lag on some phones. Show frame rate puts a small counter in the top left corner, with the frames the browser drew each second, counted over every half second. It is off at first. The settings are kept in the browser for good, like the achievements.

On a computer with a mouse, a faint full screen button sits in the top right corner and brightens under the pointer. A click puts the page in full screen, and another click or Escape leaves it. Phones and tablets do not show it, and neither does a page that the browser does not let go full screen.

The visit is saved in the browser every two seconds and whenever the page is hidden or left. When the page opens again with a saved visit, a screen says that the room waited, and offers Continue and Start over. A short line under Start over says that the achievements most likely stay, that the rest can still be earned, and that the world may change a little. Continue brings back the visit as it was left: where the keeper stood and which close-up was open, the camera mode, the sticks and the first-person look, every item where it stood, its lid open or closed, the heater and the tap on or off, what every vessel holds, the puddles and the cloth. Meanwhile the world has lived on for the time away, up to twelve hours, as `docs/simulation.md` says under Returning: tea cools or turns bitter, and a kettle on the working heater may boil dry. A pour that was running when the page closed has stopped. The light moves on to the next time of day the room offers, at a random hour within it: after dawn comes day, after day the sunset, and after the sunset dawn again. The keeper's voice and the jokes told once a visit start anew, as on every opening. A spoon that crumbled waits on the tea table again, and the caddy is full where it stands. When the spoon came back, or the caddy had been empty, the keeper says so in one line, and in one line for both. Start over forgets the saved visit and opens the room anew. The keeper's death forgets it too, so after YOU DIED a reload is a new game. When an update of the game can no longer read the saved visit, the room opens anew and the keeper says in one line that the last visit did not survive. An update that only adds or removes a bowl or a figurine keeps the visit.

Each time the room opens, it opens at dawn, by day or at sunset, chosen at random, at a random moment within that time, and the light holds still while the player stays. A new visit sets the bowls on the shelf in a new random order, and a continued visit leaves them where they were. A new game also arranges the room at random, and a continued visit keeps its arrangement. Each choice is made on its own, so any of them can meet any other. The window is either a small one in the back wall or a long one that runs the length of the left wall. The kitchen or the shelf stands beside it, whichever the game chose. By the small window, the one beside it stands on the back wall and the other on the left wall. By the long window, both stand on the back wall, with the medal and the gear between them. The tea table stands in front of the window or along an open side of the room: by the small window along the right side, and by the long window along the front side or the right side. A table along an open side has its first cushion on the long side that faces into the room, and a second cushion at its end. The caddy, the spoon and the cloth lie either in their usual places, the caddy on the shelf and the spoon and the cloth on the tea table, or apart: the caddy on the tea table, the spoon on the counter and the cloth on a middle board of the shelf. The tea table has one cushion or two, all in terracotta or all in soft blue. The room has one cloth or two, the second on the counter, and each cloth has blue stripes or a red check. The prophecy stands only above the window in the back wall. At dawn the sun stands low in the east, on the room's right, by day it stands high behind the window, and at sunset it shines low into the room through its open front on the left. At dawn and at sunset its light is weaker and warm orange, and by day it is a softer warm white, and the sky's light turns from peach to a pale warm cream with it. The same sun and sky light the garden and the room. In first person the camera stands at the keeper's eyes with a wide view, the keeper is not drawn, the carried items are held in the corners of the view as in a close-up, and a blue sky with white cumulus clouds rises over the garden, warmer at dawn and at sunset.

On the outside of the back wall, on the beam above the window, two lines are written in a playful hand. They show only from the window side of the tea table, where the close-up looks over the wall: the prophecy shall come true, one day a cherry pie will finish this tea room and it will live a life of its own, or not.

Around the house lies a lawn with tufts of grass. Tulips, daisies, marigolds and poppies grow scattered over the whole lawn, each with room of its own and a colour of its own, with rose bushes in bloom among them. Nothing grows inside the room, and the garden takes no taps.

Items in hand are drawn in the keeper's hands in the room. In a close-up, where the keeper is hidden, they are drawn in the bottom corners of the screen, the first hand on the left and the second on the right, tilted towards the viewer so a bowl's tea shows. Each is scaled to fit its corner by width and by height, so the tall thermos is no taller than a fifth of the screen. Nothing names them: the player sees what they carry. The vessel being aimed is drawn over the target, tipped by its tilt, with its stream falling straight down from the spout. A stream from a spout or the tap grows down from it as the water falls, and when it stops, its tail leaves the spout and falls after it. The sink is sunk into the counter, with pale steel sides and a darker floor. An item in it stands on that floor under the tap's spout, which is high enough to pour over the tall thermos, and its open lid sits ajar on its rim. Nothing can be put down on the counter where it would hang over the sink. While a vessel's lid is closed, the stream from the tap ends on the lid, and with nothing in the sink it ends in the sink. Once it is full, the water creeps down its side at 12 cm/s, beside the gauge towards the spout, and ends at its base, in the sink. When the heater is switched off after working two minutes or more, the keeper remarks on the electricity in one line: the kilowatt-hours it took, the tea they would have bought, or a hope that it gets switched off when leaving the house. When the tap is turned off after running two minutes or more, the keeper says in one line how many litres went down the drain since it was opened. While a vessel heats on a working plate, its water moves: a faint shimmer from 40 °C, a simmer from 55 °C and a boil from 95 °C, seen on the open kettle's water and in its gauge. Steam rises from 60 °C: from the kettle's spout, and from its opening too while its lid is open. Lifted off the plate, the water goes still, and the steam stays while the water is hot. Water is the same pale, nearly clear blue everywhere: inside the kettle, in its gauge, in a bowl and in a stream. Tea blends from it into its own colour as it grows stronger. Tea of extreme strength, brewed on a heap of leaves, turns black and opaque as tar, with an oily rainbow sheen on its surface. The surface of water and tea is glossy and reflects the room, so even clear water shows in an opaque bowl. Weak tea is see-through, so the bowl's inside, its glaze and its painting, colours what the player sees, and strong tea hides it. Each tea bowl also lends its water and its tea a share of its own colour, as a glaze does through a thin layer of liquid: the same water looks bluish in the blue bowls, greenish in the marble one, violet in the temper-coloured one and warm in the yellow and the clay bowls, and tea shifts the same way while staying as dark as it is. In the glass bowls the tea shows through the walls. The thermos is a tall Chinese vacuum flask: an aluminium foot, a glossy painted body with sakura branches over Mount Fuji at dusk, its snowy peak reflected and rippling in a lake below a line of trees, an aluminium shoulder and a ridged neck, and an aluminium cup for a lid that stands upside down beside it when open. With its lid open, its dark inside shows through the neck, and so does its water, rising as it fills from the kettle or the tap. When a pour or the tap fills it past its rim, the water runs over the lip, down the neck, over the shoulder and down the body, as it does down the kettle. The kettle has a dark glass gauge on its side, curved along its round body, where the pale water rises as it fills. With its lid open, the kettle shows its inside: dark clay when it is empty, otherwise the surface of the water or the tea, higher and wider the more it holds. Leaves tipped into it float on the surface, lying flat, two for every gram and never more than twelve, so the water still shows as it turns into tea, and they drift slowly and rock with the water. The hotter the water on the plate, the faster they turn, and at the boil they spin round. In an empty kettle they lie on its bottom, seen through the opening from above. Leaves in a tea bowl float on its tea the same way, and when the bowl is drunk or poured out they lie on its bottom. A sip from a bowl with leaves in it gets a line about the leaves, unless the tea is too hot. Items standing on furniture cast no shadows. The ten tea bowls have their own looks: white, pearl, sky blue with a crackle of fine dark lines in its glaze, deep lapis blue in a glossy ruri glaze, with paler runs where the glaze flowed down from the rim, inside and out, and a bare clay foot, once broken into shards and mended with gold in the kintsugi way: its seams run from where it struck the floor near the bottom out to the rim, over it and down the outside, three smaller shards are cut off between them, a lost chip is filled with gold where two seams meet, and the gold shines like metal, yellow, green marble with pale veins, a metal glaze in temper colours that shift from gold to violet to blue as the light moves over it, fluted clear glass that bends what is behind it and shows the tea inside, clear glass covered in small round bumps you could feel with a fingertip, with a gilded rim, and matte brown Yixing clay, porous and speckled, with 茶, tea, written in ink on its bottom, the same character in Chinese and Japanese. Each bowl has walls of real thickness and stands on a foot ring, so its inside bottom sits clear of the table. The white one has kohaku koi painted on its bottom, seen from above as a real carp is: white with red patches, a scaled back, a blunt head with eyes and barbels, fins with rays and a forked tail. Each time the room opens, its bottom shows one of four ponds, chosen at random: one red koi, two circling head to tail, two swimming together with one ahead, or two red ones side by side. Where two swim, they differ in size and in their patches, and in the first two ponds one is red and the other orange. The pearl one has a pink lotus painted on its bottom, seen from above: four rings of pointed petals with veins, blushing towards their tips, a ring of golden stamens and a green seed pod with its seeds. The yellow one has a grey heron painted on its bottom, standing on one leg in the water among reeds, with its black plume and yellow bill. The caddy is a tin with a rim, a label and a lid with a knob. Washed clean of its last leaf in the sink, it makes the keeper despair in one line: nothing left to drink, the next delivery a week away, or off to coffee then. With its lid open, it shows loose leaves heaped from the bottom, and the heap sinks as the spoon takes from it. Water poured in rises through the heap, and once it reaches the top the leaves float up with it. The spoon is pale bamboo, so it stands out on the dark shelves and on the tea table, and it shows the leaves it holds. Left on the working heater, it burns in the cloth's stages, darkening as it chars: a thread of smoke, then more smoke, then embers along the handle and the bowl, and a small flame once it is black, four fifths charred. Taken then, it crumbles: its ash falls onto the plate in the spoon's outline and fades within three seconds, and the keeper answers in one line: a jab at the player, since no spoon means no leaves, or a sob for the spoon. Taken earlier, it stays as dark as it got. The cloth is a soft linen tea towel: woven threads, a stitched hem and two blue stripes near one end, lying in a loose fold with small ripples and edges that sag onto the surface. It turns a darker grey as it takes in water, and wiping up tea turns it a little yellow. Left on the working heater, it steams while it is wet. Dry, it burns in stages as it chars: for about two seconds nothing shows, then a thread of smoke, then a dark scorch that spreads from the middle with ragged edges and more smoke, then glowing embers in the scorch, and a small flame only once it is four fifths charred. Taken off the heater while it smoulders or burns, from half charred on, it makes the keeper joke about the house burning down in one line. Washed in the sink, it turns clean again, burnt or not: a tea stain fades over ten seconds under the tap, so the soaked cloth visibly lightens from brown to grey as it is rinsed. Taking a cloth out of the sink that went in burnt, the keeper marvels at it in one line, in the keeper's own words for this device. The leaves look like the tea: thin dark green needles for sencha, rolled balls for oolong, dark brown chunks for shou puerh.

The room begins the ritual with the first tea of the catalog when it opens, until the tea can be chosen in the room.

The pour gesture maps tilt to flow: below 10° nothing pours, the flow grows linearly to full at 45°, and above 80% of full flow a tenth of the stream splashes. The presentation reports which share of the stream lands inside the target opening, because only the presentation knows the geometry.
